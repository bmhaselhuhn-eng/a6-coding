What counts as a western blot. Western blot means proteins separated on a gel, transferred to a membrane and detected with an antibody. An OxyBlot counts. A dot blot or slot blot, of RNA or of protein, does not; neither does a capillary system such as Wes or Jess, which has no membrane, or a membrane probed only with streptavidin or a lectin, which uses no antibody. Items marked 'Western blot only' do not count the same thing described for another assay in the paper: qPCR, immunofluorescence, IHC, flow cytometry, ELISA, mass spectrometry, or a gel that was stained rather than blotted. Judge the text the page shows, including any figure legend printed in it; a detail found only in a legend or supplement that is not shown is not stated. A method given only by citation ('as previously described [12]') is not stated: mark only what the text itself says. The Shortcut citation item is the exception: it records the citation phrase itself. Some PDFs drop the micro sign or print it as m ('50 g', '25 mg' for micrograms); rule on the intended unit.

Every question carries its own What to mark rule with a worked example. Follow it rather than your instinct: consistency between reviewers matters more here than any single choice. Full rules under Guide.

Antibody diluent
What an antibody was diluted in for the blot: a named diluent, a plain buffer such as PBS-T, or a recipe such as 5% BSA in TBST. 'In blocking buffer' counts when the blocking buffer's composition is stated. A diluent for IHC, IF or flow cytometry does not count. Western blot only.
What to mark. Mark the diluent words, not the dilution.In primary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA/TBST mark 5% BSA/TBST. Nothing to mark in an IHC antibody diluted in PBS.

Effects of Food-Processing Treatments on Solubility and Molecular Profiles of Treated-Nut Proteins Tree nuts are commonly associated with food-allergy-related adverse reactions and anaphylaxis. Accordingly, this study investigated the effects of thermal treatments such as soaking and extensive boiling on the protein structure integrity, solubility, and allergenic Foods 2024, 13, 3440 6 of 14 properties of cashew and pistachio allergens. By subjecting these tree nut proteins to controlled thermal processes, we can understand the essentials for developing safer allergy management strategies and could contribute to reducing the health risks associated with nut consumption. 3.1.1. Effects of Soaking on the Solubility and Profile of Proteins in Cashew and Pistachio Kernels Processing allergenic ingredients through wet thermal processing such as soaking is typically intended to soften the seed by increasing its water uptake and removing low-molecular- weight allergens [20]. Accordingly, the effects of soaking at room temperature versus an elevated temperature, 37 ◦C, on the solubility and extractability of cashew and pistachio proteins were studied. The protein concentrations of the extracts of raw, soaked, and roasted cashew and pistachio kernels are presented in Figure 1A. The protein profiles of soaking-treated cashew and pistachio kernel proteins are presented in Figures 2A and 2B, respectively. As apparent from Figure 1A, the soaking temperature and time had no significant effects on the extractable protein concentrations in both cashew and pistachio kernels. Overall, raw nuts exhibited the highest soluble protein concentrations (19.2 mg/mL and 23.8 mg/mL for cashew and pistachio, respectively) in contrast to the roasted nuts, which exhibited the lowest soluble protein concentrations in both cashew and pistachio (14 mg/mL and 18 mg/mL, respectively). Interestingly, neither soaking time nor temperature significantly impacted the soluble protein concentrations in cashew kernels (i.e., 18.8 mg/mL when soaked for 2 h at RT and 18.6 mg/mL when soaked for 0.5 h at 37 ◦C). On the other hand, pistachio kernels showed markedly decreased extractable protein concentrations with soaking time (i.e., 23.5 mg/mL when soaked for 0.5 h at RT versus 18.7 mg/mL when soaked for 2 h at RT). However, soaking temperature did not impact the soluble protein concentrations (i.e., 22.4 mg/mL when soaked for 0.5 h at 37 ◦C). Effects of Boiling on the Solubility and Profile of Proteins in Cashew and Pistachio Kernels The extended boiling (1-4 h) induced remarkable reductions in the extractable protein concentrations for both cashew and pistachio in the current study (Figure 1B). In particular, the prolonged boiling at high temperatures reduced the solubility of cashew and pistachio proteins. For both cashews and pistachios, the soluble protein concentration decreased from 19.2 mg/mL and 23.8 mg/mL (raw) to 2.3 mg/mL and 4.4 mg/mL, respectively, when extensively boiled for 4 h (Figure 1B). According to Cuadrado et al., higher protein degradation is achieved when enzymatic hydrolysis after pressured heating is applied in both nuts [16,18]. Compared to the cashew nuts, which exhibited a significant decrease in soluble protein concentrations proportional to boiling time (i.e., from 19.2 mg/mL (raw) to 11.2 mg/mL, 9 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL when boiled for 0.5 h, 1 h, and 2 h, respectively), the pistachio exhibited a more stable protein concentration between boiling times up to 2 h (12.5 mg/mL for 0.5 h and 11 mg/mL for 2 h). Beyond 2 h, there was a significant reduction in soluble proteins (4.4 mg/mL), suggesting pistachio proteins were more resistant to heat. As with the soaking samples, the protein profiles of the boiled-water samples of cashew and pistachio and raw kernel extracts were characterized using SDS-PAGE (Figure 2C,D). As demonstrated in Figure 2C, the disappearance of Ana o 1 and Ana o 2 bands illustrated that the extensive boiling of cashew kernels for 4 h significantly reduced the extractable protein probably due to decreased protein solubility of 7S globulin and 11S globulin. The concentration of Ana o 3 in boiled kernels was proportionally decreased with boiling time (Figure 2C), which was likely due to structural modifications such as denaturation, aggregation, or reactions with food components. In line with this, the boiled water contained increasing Ana o 3 concentrations from leaching of this allergen into the boiling water, reaching its highest quantity at 4 h. This was in line with previous studies that showed soluble protein leakage from nut kernels into the treated water when peanuts were boiled [21,22]. Furthermore, the most distinct protein bands, approximately 33 kDa (Pis v 5) and 13 kDa (Pis v 1), were observed when the pistachio kernels were boiled for 0.5 h (Figure 2D), similar to previously reported studies [23,24]. Most of the pistachio proteins were resistant to the boiling treatment up to 2 h, indicated by a protein profile similar to that of raw pistachio. Of the two Pis v 5 bands, the higher molecular band was susceptible to boiling from 0.5 h and completely disappeared by 2 h. The lower molecular band of Pis v 5, Pis v 2, and Pis v 4 were generally Foods 2024, 13, 3440 8 of 14 resistant to boiling for up to 2 h. The boiling treatment also caused protein fragmentation in both cashew and pistachio samples. Similarly to cashew Ana o 3, Pis v 1 also showed leaching effects proportional to boiling time. Boiling for 4 h induced degradation of cashew and pistachio proteins, with high-molecular-weight proteins degrading into low-molecular-weight forms. Conclusions In the current study, extended boiling up to 4 h modified the solubility, conformational structure, as well as dramatically reduced the polyclonal IgG- and sIgE-binding capacity of allergens in both cashew and pistachio nuts. Short soaking did not show any signifi- cant effects on the IgG- and IgE-binding capacity of the allergens. Roasting appeared to increase the IgE-binding capacity of allergens in both cashews and pistachios. Boiling the cashew and pistachio kernels effectively removed significant quantities of highly potent 2S albumins from the nuts, gradually reducing their allergenic potential with longer boiling times, resulting in progressively lower allergenicity. Pistachio proteins appeared to be more sensitive to boiling than cashew proteins. The boiling regime (i.e., boiling for 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h) has potential for use in cashew and pistachio oral immunotherapy. Additionally, the cross-reactivity between cashew and pistachio, and their processing-induced allergen modification demonstrated in this study, suggest that boiled pistachio proteins with even lower allergenic potential could be used as a cashew substitute in cashew OIT.                       You do not type anything

                      Select the words in the Methods text, then press Use highlighted text. The exact words you selected are what gets recorded.

                      Typing is what this design removes. Given a sentence like the one below, one person types RIPA, another types the whole parenthesis, a third types the clause. All three read the paper correctly and disagree in the data. Selecting removes that entirely.

                      How much to select

                      The shortest phrase that states the detail. Not the whole sentence when three words will do, and not the vendor, city or catalog number unless the question asks for them.

                      Every question shows a worked example under What to mark. Follow it rather than your instinct.

                      When a paper gives several

                      Many papers list a table of antibodies with eight RRIDs, eight catalog numbers and several vendors. Mark them all. Press Use highlighted text once per value and they stack up in a numbered list. Press Done, next when the list is complete.

                      If they sit in a contiguous table or a run-on list, selecting the whole block in one go is fine and much faster. You are marking where the evidence is, not counting.

                      The same value repeated does not need marking twice. A paper that uses 1:1000 for six antibodies has one dilution, not six.

                      If you stop before you have them all, tick 'There are more of these than I marked'. That box matters more than it looks: without it, someone who stopped at five of eight cannot be told apart from someone who found everything, and the three they skipped would count against the extractor as mistakes it did not make.

                      What counts

                      Judge only what is written. Not what the authors almost certainly did, and not what is standard practice in the field.

                      Judge the text shown. The page shows the Methods section, and sometimes figure legends or the full text where no Methods heading was found. A detail in the text shown counts, legend included. A detail found only in a legend or supplementary file that is not shown is Not stated.

                      Unclear is a real answer. It is set aside rather than counted either way, so use it when you are genuinely stuck rather than guessing.

                      The shaded sentences

                      The faint tint marks the western blot section: sentences that name the blot, the gel, the membrane or its detection. It is the same for every question on a paper. Blot section jumps between its blocks. Other assays are left untinted, but an item can still be stated elsewhere, for example in a figure legend.

                      The warmer marks are the sentences most likely to hold the answer to the open question, and Prev and Next step between them.

                      Both are search aids and nothing more. Fixed keyword rules pick them; they are never told the answer, they sometimes shade irrelevant sentences and sometimes miss the right one. Read around them, and judge only the text.

An isotype that names what a secondary recognizes ('goat anti-rabbit IgG'), an isotype control, a normal or control IgG, or a serum antibody response does not count. Host species and clonality belong to Primary antibody host or type. Counts for an antibody used in any assay, not only the blot.
                      Mark: Mark each distinct isotype once. In mouse monoclonal IgG2a anti-GFAP mark IgG2a; in rabbit polyclonal IgG anti-Kif5 mark IgG. Nothing to mark in HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG.
                      Total protein stainA total-protein stain used to normalize or check the blot: Ponceau S or REVERT on the membrane, stain-free imaging used for normalization, or Coomassie stated as the blot's loading or transfer check, including a parallel gel stained and stated as the blot's loading check. A stained gel with no stated link to a blot, or a stain-free gel named only as a product, does not count. Western blot only.
                      Mark: Mark the stain name only. In confirmed by Ponceau S staining mark Ponceau S. Nothing to mark in run on a 4-15% TGX Stain-Free gel with no normalization stated, in any stain on a purity, expression, preparative, mass-spec or antibody-QC gel, or in a stain on a gel that was never blotted and is not stated as the blot's loading check.
                      Blinded quantification statedA statement that the blot's band quantification or image analysis was done blind to group or treatment. Blinding of another assay, of animal allocation, or of the study as a whole without naming the blot analysis does not count. Western blot only.
                      Mark: Mark the statement. In densitometry was performed by an investigator blinded to treatment group mark the whole phrase. Nothing to mark in histology was scored by a blinded pathologist.
                      Secondary antibody host or targetThe host species of a secondary antibody, or the species it recognizes, such as goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG. The conjugate or vendor alone does not count, and neither does a primary antibody's host. Counts for an antibody used in any assay, not only the blot.
                      Mark: Mark the host and target together where they sit together. Mark each distinct combination once. In HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG mark goat anti-rabbit.
